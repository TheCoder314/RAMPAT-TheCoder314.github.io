One set of rotors for hover and cruise

Most VTOL drones carry separate motors for hovering and for forward flight. R.A.M.P.A.T. tilts the same three rotors between both, which cuts weight and cost.

A servo-driven tilt mechanism in each nacelle sets the motor angle for each flight mode. Tiltrotors exist, but this is the first 3D-printed tiltrotor built for disaster response.

HoverRotors up for vertical takeoff and landing
TransitionRotors tilt as the wing builds lift
CruiseRotors forward, flying on the wing

How the design got here

Three airframes, each rebuilt around what the last one got wrong.

  1. Foam and cardboard

    A low-cost VTOL built from foam and cardboard. It was too fragile to fly stably, but it proved the concept was worth pursuing.

    Proof of concept

  2. Modular 3D-printed parts

    The airframe moved to modular printed parts. Testing showed a misaligned center of gravity, high drag, and structural instability from the wooden spars.

    Airframe and aerodynamics needed a full revision

  3. Carbon fiber and CFD

    Wooden spars and booms became carbon fiber for strength, weight and correct balance. CFD drove a streamlined fuselage and a V-tail, and a part-slotting system holds it all together.

    Stable structure and flight performance

Why it exists

In the past decade, more than 400,000 people died in natural disasters, most of them in developing countries. Aerial reconnaissance helps first responders find survivors and assess damage, but capable drones cost over $10,000.

Built to be repaired

Parts slot together along the carbon tubes, so a damaged section comes off and a new print goes on. A crash becomes a quick reprint instead of a lost aircraft.

The wing is split into panels on a carbon fiber spar for stiffness. The three-part fuselage holds the battery, PCB and thermal camera.

PCB design

A custom flight controller for $75.

Off-the-shelf flight controllers with the features this aircraft needs cost over $500. A custom board brought that down by 85%, using off-the-shelf sensors and custom control software.

How the board was designed

From requirements to a logged flight.

  1. Set the requirements

    The tiltrotor's design and its sub-$1,000 budget ruled out commercial controllers. The board needed:

    • Full sensor integration
    • Long-range radio telemetry
    • High-frequency flight stabilization
    • Onboard data logging to microSD

  2. Lay it out in KiCad

    The schematic and layout were drawn in KiCad 9 on a four-layer, 122.5 × 61.5 mm board, with traces routed between the microcontroller, sensors, radio, servo outputs and ESC outputs.

    The Gerber and drill files exported from this layout are what you upload to a PCB fab.

  3. Fabricate and assemble

    The board was fabricated, then populated with off-the-shelf sensor and radio modules. Keeping the modules standard means they can be swapped if one fails.

  4. Write the control loop

    The firmware reads the IMU, fuses the data into an attitude estimate, compares it to the desired position, and drives the motors and servos through PID control and control mixing.

CFD

Shaped by airflow simulation.

The airframe went through seven geometries in SimScale. Each round cut high-pressure areas on the front of the aircraft and increased low pressure over the top of the wing.

Flight test procedure

Repeated 15 times to measure variance.

  1. Preflight

    Install the battery, power the avionics, and check telemetry and control surfaces. Arm and confirm hover, transition and fixed-wing modes.

  2. Hover

    Take off vertically and hold altitude for 5 minutes, logging current draw and gyroscope data.

  3. Transition

    Climb to 50 ft, enter transition mode and hold throttle for 2 minutes, logging gyroscope and airspeed.

  4. Cruise

    Switch to fixed-wing mode and cruise for 10 minutes, logging gyroscope, airspeed and power.

  5. Recovery

    Transition back to hover, land vertically, then disarm and power down.

What comes next
